DeepSeek just opened its search engine. Every page now gets an authority score.

A major AI lab now shows a page-level authority tier for every search result, and you can check your own site for under a cent. Here is what the tiers are, what DeepSeek has not said and what is worth doing this month.

By AIkonX Team20 October 2026 · 7 minute read

On 17 October 2026, DeepSeek opened the search engine behind its own chat product to developers. The new Search API returns ranked web results, and every result carries an authority tier and a quality score. Search Engine Journal reported the launch on 19 October. The detail we rely on here comes from DeepSeek's own documentation.

For an Australian business the point is simple. Until now, nobody outside an AI lab could see how that lab rates a page. With this API, anyone with a key can look up their own domain and read the result. You no longer have to guess whether an AI search product can see your pages, because you can ask it. What you cannot yet find out is why a page scored the way it did, and we will be plain about that gap throughout.

What DeepSeek actually launched

There are two tools. The Search API returns results for a query. A companion Fetch API retrieves a specific page and hands it back as Markdown. DeepSeek says the same engine already powers search on its website and in its API, that it indexes hundreds of billions of web pages and that it handles hundreds of millions of search requests a day. Those are DeepSeek's own figures, and we have no independent way to check them.

The changelog also makes a claim about quality. It says the engine filters out SEO spam, "GEO manipulation" and low-quality AI-generated content, and it credits strict detection for that. The changelog does not define GEO manipulation and does not explain how the detection works. We come back to that below.

What a search result contains

Each result is more than a link and a title. It has two scores, two pieces of text written for different readers and, when you ask for it, the full page. The display snippet is short, usually 100 to 200 characters, and is meant for a person. The summary is longer, usually 600 to 800 characters, and is meant for the language model reading the results. Full page content comes back when you request it and when it is available. Queries can use site:, a language filter and a published-date filter.

Authority tiers and rerank scores

The authority field runs from 0 to 4, and −1 means the engine does not know. The API reference calls tier 3 and above "highly authoritative". The rerank score is separate. It runs from 0 to 3 and blends relevance to the query, authority and freshness, so the same page can score differently for different searches. A score of 1.5 or above counts as high quality.

The important caveat is that neither the guide nor the reference explains how tiers or scores are assigned. We do not know what signals feed a tier, whether it is judged per page or partly per site, or how often it changes. Any article that tells you how to reach tier 4 is guessing, and this one will not.

What "GEO manipulation" probably means

This section is our interpretation, not DeepSeek's. DeepSeek has not defined the term. In plain terms, we read it as content produced to win citations from AI systems rather than to help a reader: pages stuffed with the exact phrasing a model might quote, near-duplicate pages across dozens of suburbs, or bulk text with nothing original in it.

The reading is plausible because Google said something similar. On 15 May 2026 it stated that its spam policies also apply to generative AI responses in Google Search. Two very different companies have now said, in public, that tactics aimed at gaming AI answers are unwelcome. The practical takeaway is that citation-gaming tricks carry more risk than they did a year ago. Google has been busy policing its own results too, as we covered in our look at Google SERP poisoning. The crawler and robots.txt question

Many site owners will want to know which bot to allow or block. DeepSeek has not said. Its EU training-data summary for DeepSeek-V4 says the crawler is designed to respect robots.txt where applicable, but it does not name the crawler. None of the launch pages name the crawler behind the search index, or the user agent that Fetch uses. Fetch itself is described as subject to the target site's availability and access policies.

The Known Agents directory lists a user agent called DeepSeekBot and describes it as an AI training data crawler. That suggests blocking DeepSeekBot in robots.txt may only affect training, and may not change what appears in DeepSeek's search index. We want to be clear that this is unconfirmed. It is a reason not to assume a robots.txt rule does what you expect, not a finding.

Pricing and the 31 October billing date

Search and Fetch each cost US$0.75 per 1,000 successful requests. Empty results are not billed, and asking for 50 results costs the same as asking for one. A site: check on your own domain therefore costs under one cent and returns up to 50 of your pages, each with a tier and a relevance score.

There is a date to note. Server-side searches and fetches made through coding tools such as Claude Code and Codex with the DeepSeek API are free until billing starts on 31 October 2026 at 16:00 UTC. In Melbourne (AEDT) that is 3:00am on 1 November.

What we know and what we do not

One more piece of context, kept short. DeepSeek's privacy policy, revised on 10 February, mentions using third-party search APIs. Bocha, a Chinese search API company, says in a GitHub README that it is DeepSeek's official web search supplier. DeepSeek's launch pages do not mention Bocha, and it is unclear whether any queries still go to a third party.

There is also a trap in the site: check. If one of your pages does not appear, it could be outside the index, caught by the quality filters or excluded for some other reason. The API offers no way to tell which. A missing page is a prompt to investigate, not a verdict.

What to do this month

None of this needs a large project. Most of it can be done in an afternoon, and the first step costs next to nothing.

  1. Run a site: query against your domain. Record which pages come back and the tier on each. Save the output with the date so you can compare later.
  2. Note important pages that are missing. Remember the API cannot tell you why, so check those pages for thin content, duplicated text or access problems before drawing conclusions.
  3. Review your robots.txt rules for DeepSeekBot. Understand they may only affect training, not search. Do not rely on them to control what DeepSeek's search shows.
  4. Cut thin or AI-padded pages that exist only to get cited. Merge them into one useful page or remove them.
  5. Strengthen the pages that should carry authority. Original data, named authors and clear sourcing are good for readers whatever any scoring system does. Our SEO team can help with an audit.
  6. Watch DeepSeek's docs. The three things to look for are a definition of GEO manipulation, a tier methodology and a named index crawler.
