Google's September 2026 spam update checklist: what to check before you change anything

Google's September 2026 spam update has finished. Google's Search Status Dashboard says it was released on 24 September 2026 and the rollout was complete as of 8 October 2026. If your enquiries or organic traffic moved around those dates, check your own data first. Don't rewrite your site yet. This checklist shows what to check, in order.

By AIkonX13 November 2026

Nobody can tell from outside whether this update affected your site, and nobody can promise what it means for your rankings. What you can do is work out, with your own numbers, whether it is worth acting.

What did Google say about the September 2026 spam update?

Google's Search Status Dashboard logs two entries for the update, in US Pacific time:

  • 24 September 2026, 9:15 am PDT: "Released the September 2026 spam update, which applies globally and to all languages. The rollout may take up to two weeks to complete." In Melbourne that was about 2:15 am on Friday 25 September.
  • 8 October 2026, 1:37 am PDT: "The rollout was complete as of October 8, 2026." In Melbourne that was about 7:30 pm on 8 October.

Search Engine Roundtable's report of 8 October 2026 adds a few more details, with Barry Schwartz as the author:

  • It quotes Google on LinkedIn: "This is a normal spam update, and it will roll out for all languages and locations."
  • It says this update took about two weeks, while the spam updates before it took about two days.
  • It says Google would not tell it what share of queries or searches were affected.
  • Its quick facts say the update does not target link spam and does not target every spam policy. It also says Google did not mention any changes with this update.
  • It says Google told it that recovery can take many months, and that Google will do periodic refreshes of the update.

Neither Google's dashboard entry nor Search Engine Roundtable's report says which spam policies this update focused on. If a website tells you exactly which tactics were hit, treat that as a guess.

Schwartz also listed the dates he documented as "impact dates": 25 to 27 September, 30 September, and 4 to 6 October. He noted that this assumes Google ran no other updates during the rollout, which he said he did not know for sure. Those dates describe the wider industry. They don't describe your site.

How do I check the dates in Search Console?

Start with data you own. Open the Performance report in Google Search Console and set three date ranges:

  1. Before: the two weeks before 24 September.
  2. During: 24 September to 8 October.
  3. After: the most recent two weeks you have.

Compare clicks, impressions and average position across the three. Use the same number of days in each range, and the same mix of weekdays and weekends where you can, because many businesses have weekly swings. For a cleaner read, compare against the same dates last year as well.

Google's dashboard uses US Pacific time, and Search Console reports by day. A change on one day won't line up with the exact hour the rollout started or finished. Look for a pattern across several days, not a single data point.

How do I tell a spam update from other causes?

A dip near the update dates is a reason to investigate. It isn't proof. Rule out the other explanations first:

  • Tracking problems. Did a tag, a cookie banner or a site change stop your analytics recording visits? Check that Search Console and your analytics tool tell the same story. If Search Console impressions held steady but analytics sessions fell, suspect tracking.
  • Seasonality. Many Australian businesses have predictable busy and quiet periods, such as school holidays, end of financial year or the lead-up to Christmas. Compare with last year.
  • Technical issues. Look at the Pages (indexing) report for new errors, pages marked noindex by mistake, a changed robots.txt file, slow servers, or a redirect rule that broke during a site update.
  • Other Google changes. Check Google's official list of Search ranking updates for anything that overlaps your dates. If two updates overlap, you can't credit one of them with a change.
  • Your own work. Did you launch, delete or restructure pages, change titles, or pause content or ad campaigns in the same window?
  • Rank tracker noise. If the only evidence is a rank tracker, verify it in Search Console before you act. Our post on Google ranking drops and poisoned results pages explains how rank tracker data can mislead.

Write down what you find for each item, even when the answer is "nothing". It saves repeat work later.

Did the change hit the whole site or a few pages?

If the numbers did move, narrow it down. In the Performance report, switch between Pages, Queries, Countries and Devices. Ask:

  • Is the change spread across the site, or concentrated in one folder, one template or one type of page?
  • Does it affect branded searches (your business name), non-branded searches, or both?
  • Is it only Australia, or every country you get visits from?

A pattern gives you something to act on. A small wobble across everything often points to causes that aren't specific to your pages.

How do I review my site against Google's spam policies?

Search Engine Roundtable quotes Google's spam update documentation: "Sites that see a change after a spam update should review our spam policies to ensure they are complying with those. Sites that violate our policies may rank lower in results or not appear in results at all."

So read Google's spam policies page from top to bottom, and ask of each policy: does anything on our site, or anything we pay others to do, look like this?

Three policies are worth a close look. Google introduced them in March 2024 in a Search Central blog post, which describes them like this:

  • Scaled content abuse: many pages generated for the primary purpose of manipulating Search rankings and not helping users. Google says this applies whether the content comes from automation, humans or a mix of both.
  • Site reputation abuse: third-party pages published with little or no first-party oversight or involvement, where the purpose is to manipulate Search rankings by taking advantage of the host site's ranking signals. Google says it doesn't treat all third-party content as a violation.
  • Expired domain abuse: an expired domain name bought and repurposed primarily to manipulate Search rankings by hosting content that provides little to no value to users. Google says it's fine to use an old domain name for a new, original site designed to serve people first.

Neither Google's dashboard nor Search Engine Roundtable says this update targeted any of these three. They are a sensible starting point because they are policies Google has explained in plain terms, not because we know they were involved.

Practical places to look:

  • Content made in bulk. Lists of near-identical suburb pages, thin pages produced from a template, or large batches of articles published mainly to catch searches rather than help a reader.
  • Work from outside suppliers. Anything an agency, freelancer or tool published for you, including old work from a previous provider.
  • Third-party sections on your domain. Sponsored, partner or other pages that have little to do with what your site is about and get little oversight from you.
  • Anything you inherited. An old domain, a previous agency's tactics or a site you bought.
  • Hidden or misleading elements. Text or links hidden from visitors, or pages that show search engines something different from what people see.
  • User-generated areas. Comments, forums or reviews where visitors have posted spam.

Be honest and specific. A page that exists because a customer needs it is in a different position from a page that exists to attract a search. If you aren't sure where a page sits, flag it for a closer look. Don't delete it on a hunch.

What should I avoid doing right now?

Reactions often cost more than the update itself. Hold off on:

  • Deleting large numbers of pages before you know which ones, if any, are a problem.
  • Rewriting your whole site because a rank tracker turned red.
  • Disavowing links in bulk without evidence that they are an issue.
  • Switching agencies, platforms or domains mid-investigation.
  • Promising your boss or your clients a recovery date.

Google's spam update documentation, as quoted by Search Engine Roundtable, says making changes "may help a site improve if our automated systems learn over a period of months that the site complies with our spam policies." That is Google's general statement. It is not a forecast for your site, and we won't offer one.

What should I do this week?

A reasonable plan for a small business or marketing team:

  1. Today: Set up the before, during and after comparison in Search Console and save a copy for your records.
  2. Day two: Rule out tracking, seasonality, technical issues and your own site changes using the list above.
  3. Day three: Narrow down which pages, searches and countries moved, if any.
  4. This week: Read Google's spam policies and mark any pages or practices that need a closer look.
  5. Next: Fix real problems in order of risk, one at a time, and note the date of each change so you can read your data later.
  6. Ongoing: Re-check in a few weeks. Google says systems may learn about changes over months, so one snapshot won't tell the full story.

FAQ

When did Google's September 2026 spam update start and finish?

Google's Search Status Dashboard says it was released on 24 September 2026 at 9:15 am US Pacific time. It says the rollout was complete as of 8 October 2026.

Does the update apply to Australia?

Google's dashboard entry says the update applies globally and to all languages. Search Engine Roundtable quotes Google as saying it rolls out for all languages and locations.

Which spam policies did Google target?

Google hasn't said in its dashboard entry or in Search Engine Roundtable's report. That report's quick facts say the update doesn't target link spam and doesn't target every policy. That is why this checklist asks you to review your site against Google's full spam policies page.

My traffic dropped. Was it the spam update?

You can't tell from the dates alone. Compare the before, during and after periods in Search Console, then rule out tracking faults, seasonality, technical issues, your own changes and other Google updates before you decide on a cause.

Should I delete content straight away?

No. Find out which pages, if any, are affected, and review them against Google's spam policies first. Deleting pages in a hurry can remove content that was doing its job.

How long does recovery take?

Nobody can say for your site. Search Engine Roundtable reports that Google said it can take many months, and that Google will do periodic refreshes. Treat that as a general statement from Google, not a timeline.
