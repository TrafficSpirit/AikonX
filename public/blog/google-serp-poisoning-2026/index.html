Google SERP poisoning in 2026: what the data shows

Since September, Google has been handing automated search requests complete, normal looking results pages with the wrong websites on them. Here is the evidence, what it does to rank tracking, and how to tell a real drop from bad data.

17 October 2026 · 9 minute read

If your rank tracker showed a sudden collapse in late September 2026 and a recovery a few days later, your website probably did nothing wrong. A growing body of evidence shows Google has been answering a share of automated search requests with results pages that look completely normal but list the wrong sites. The search industry now calls these poisoned SERPs.

This article brings together the published data, mainly a 27.5 million SERP study released by rank tracking company Nozzle on 10 October 2026, along with supporting reports from DemandSphere, Search Engine Journal, Search Engine Roundtable and PPC Land. We also cover the older and more dangerous meaning of SERP poisoning, where criminals push malware sites to the top of Google, because that has been unusually active this year too.

What a poisoned SERP actually is

Nozzle defines a poisoned SERP as a results page where fewer than half of a keyword's usual websites appear on any page, and those websites come back on a later check. The usual sites are the ones that held a top 10 position on at least 60% of that keyword's checks between 20 August and 9 September 2026.

What separates this from ordinary blocking is that nothing looks broken. There is no error and no captcha. The page uses the same template and the same build of Google's front end as a normal page. A blocked request tells a tool to try again. A poisoned request gets stored as if it were real, and that is where the damage to reporting comes from.

Nozzle's clearest example is the US desktop query white label local seo. On 4 October page one listed SEO reseller vendors such as BrightLocal, Synup and SEOReseller. On 7 October all nine of those sites had gone. In their place were a WordPress development tool, a steakhouse in Jackson Hole, a directory of county health departments, USA.gov and several US state government sites. Google appears to have read the word "local" literally, and pagination shrank from 10 pages to 3.

How it unfolded

The order of events is the reason researchers call this deliberate poisoning rather than a glitch. On 13 September requests to Google started failing in large numbers. For about a week, the requests that got through returned normal pages. From 20 September, the ones that got through started coming back poisoned.

Since 1 October the rate has settled between roughly 5% and 11% a day. It also depends on the route a request takes to Google. Between 30 September and 9 October, one of Nozzle's collection routes returned 1% to 7% poisoned pages while another returned 9% to 30% on the same days.

Nozzle is not alone. DemandSphere reported on 7 October that Google had started returning what it calls spurious results to suspected bot traffic, on about 11% of its daily SERPs. Two vendors measuring different keyword sets with different methods landed in the same range, which makes the finding hard to wave away.

Why Australian businesses should pay attention

The effect is not spread evenly. Australia ran at 11.5%, well above the United States at 8.5% and almost four times the United Kingdom at 3.0%. If your rankings are tracked against Australian results, your September and October reports are more exposed than the global average.

Device barely matters: 7.7% on desktop, 8.3% on iPhone and 7.1% on Android. Search type does. "Near me" searches were poisoned 11.4% of the time, against 7.8% for everything else. Long tail phrases with fewer than 100 monthly searches ran at 9.4%, while brand and navigational searches were hit least, at 5.3%.

Which industries were hit hardest

Every industry peaked between 27 and 29 September, but the height of the peak varied a lot. Legal keywords were the worst, reaching 51.3% poisoned on 27 September. Home services hit 48.4% on 29 September. These are exactly the verticals where local rankings drive enquiries, so a false drop can trigger expensive decisions.

What changes on a poisoned page

The organic listings take the biggest hit. On a normal day a results page keeps 88.5% of the sites it showed on the previous check. On a poisoned page it keeps only 20.7%. "People also search for" and "People also ask" are heavily disrupted too. The one part of the page that holds steady is the part advertisers pay for: shopping results barely move, and ads actually become more consistent than usual.

The layout shifts as well. AI Overviews appeared on 34.9% of poisoned pages against 54.2% normally, and image packs nearly doubled from 23.0% to 44.2%. On desktop keywords that usually run to page 8 or beyond, 50.9% of poisoned pages stopped at page 4 or earlier.

Who fills the empty slots

Social platforms and forums. Reddit gained the most, appearing in the top 10 of 28.8% of poisoned pages against 20.8% of the same keywords' other pages. Facebook, LinkedIn, Instagram and Quora also appeared more often, while YouTube, Yelp and MapQuest appeared less.

Why Google is doing this

Google has not commented publicly. The working explanation across the industry is that the real target is AI companies rather than SEO tools. AI answer engines collect Google results in bulk to ground their responses, and Google has been tightening its defences against that all year. DemandSphere founder Ray Grieselhuber has argued that Google does not want AI labs using its results pages for live retrieval, and Barry Schwartz of Search Engine Roundtable reached a similar view, pointing to AI bots and SerpApi as the likely targets. Rank trackers collect pages the same way, so they are caught in the net.

The poisoning is the latest step in a longer run of moves against automated collection:

The overlap problem. The September spam update ran during exactly the same weeks as the worst poisoning. A genuine spam demotion and a poisoned data point look identical on a rank tracking dashboard. Search Console also fell behind in the same period, running more than 64 hours late on 11 October before catching up on 12 October.

How to tell a real ranking drop from poisoned data

Based on Nozzle's findings and the guidance published alongside them, these checks separate most real problems from bad data:

  1. Check Search Console first. If a page vanished in your tracker but Search Console still shows impressions and clicks for that query, nothing happened to your site.
  2. Wait three days. 80% of poisoning episodes last a single check and 77.9% recover within three days. A real ranking change stays.
  3. Compare against the keyword's history, not yesterday. Yesterday's check may also have been poisoned. Look at which sites usually hold page one over several weeks.
  4. Look at the whole page. If most competitors disappeared at once and Reddit, Facebook or government sites took over, the page itself is suspect.
  5. Annotate September and October reports. Any report for this period showing a sudden top 10 loss should carry a data quality note before anyone acts on it.
  6. Ask your tool vendor for error and sampling rates. DemandSphere has started publishing these. Most vendors have not yet.
  7. Spot check by hand. A private browser window is still a sensible check for a handful of important terms.

The other SERP poisoning: criminals at the top of Google

Before rank trackers adopted the term, SERP poisoning (also called SEO poisoning) meant attackers manipulating rankings so their fake download pages appear above the real ones. That has been very active in 2026, and it has started reaching AI chatbot answers as well.

Two trends stand out. Attackers are going after technical users: developers, sysadmins and people installing AI coding tools. And Microsoft observed in April 2026 that some victims reached malicious domains after asking AI assistants for download links, which pushes classic SEO poisoning into AI answers.

The advice for everyday users has not changed. Download software only from the vendor's official domain, bookmark tools you install often, read the address carefully before clicking, and treat sponsored results for software downloads with extra suspicion.

What this means for your business

For marketing teams, the lesson from September and October 2026 is that rank data is now an estimate with a measurable error rate, not ground truth. Google's own data in Search Console is the reference point, third party trackers are a useful signal, and any sudden one day change deserves a second look before it drives a budget or strategy decision.

For website owners, the criminal side is a reminder to watch your brand in search. If attackers impersonate your product, a look alike domain can outrank you for a while. Checking your branded searches for unfamiliar domains is a sensible habit.
